Today is the other half

Every equation so far was handed to you already written.

Today you write it. From a paragraph of English about a tank, or a cup of coffee.

Setting it up is the part that is graded, and the part that is hard.

↳ every equation today turns out to be linear. You already know how to finish.

Modeling, in three steps

  1. Build. Turn the words into an equation and an initial condition.
  2. Solve. This is the part you already know.
  3. Interpret. Answer the question that was actually asked, with units.

Step 2 is usually the shortest of the three.

And step 1 always uses one law

rate of change = rate in − rate out

Salt, water, heat, money. Same sentence every time.

But before you can write it down you have to decide what is changing. That is a real decision, and it is not automatic.

↳ so: choose the quantity, then fill in the two brackets. In that order.

The stirred tank

Salt water flows in. The tank is stirred, so it is the same everywhere. Mixture flows out.

It holds 100 gallons, and Q(t) pounds of salt at time t. That is what we are after.

Rate in

(3 gal/min)(¼ lb/gal) = 34 lb/min

Rate out — and this is the one people get wrong

(3 gal/min)(Q100 lb/gal) = 3Q100 lb/min

dQdt = 34 − 3Q100

↳ what leaves carries the concentration inside the tank, not the one coming in.

The most common mistake, and how to catch it

Three versions students write

dQdt = 34 − 3Q  wrong

dQdt = 34 − 34  wrong

dQdt = 34 − 3Q100  right

Check the units and the first one dies

3Q is (gal/min)(lb) = gal · lbmin  not lb/min

The second one has correct units, so units will not save you there.

It fails on meaning: it says the tank never changes, whatever is inside it.

Units killed the first one. Only meaning kills the second.

↳ so you need both. Units are the quick one, and they cost nothing.

That was a habit, not a trick

Look back at what we did before any calculus at all.

  1. We named the quantity. Q(t), the pounds of salt in the tank at time t.
  2. We said when t = 0 and what was true then. Pure water, so Q(0) = 0.
  3. We asked what V was doing. Sitting at 100 gallons this time. It will not always.

Only then did we write the two brackets.

And the first one is a choice. A different choice gives a different equation for the same tank.

Track the concentration instead

c = Q100:   dcdt = 3400 − 3c100

Neither one is wrong. That one settles at ¼ lb/gal — the concentration coming in, which is obviously right.

Write “Let Q(t) be …” on your paper. One line.

↳ the units arrive with the name. They are not a separate rule to remember.

Example 1 — now solve it

dQdt = 34 − 3Q100,   Q(0) = 0  the tank starts as pure water

Standard form — it is linear

Q′ + 0.03 Q = 0.75  p = 0.03, a constant

1  build μ

μ = e∫0.03 dt = e0.03t

2  multiply every term

e0.03tQ′ + 0.03 e0.03tQ = 0.75 e0.03t

3  the left side is one derivative

(e0.03tQ)′ = 0.75 e0.03t

4  integrate, then divide by μ

e0.03tQ = 25 e0.03t + c  0.75/0.03 = 25

Q = 25 + c e−0.03t

Q(0) = 0:   0 = 25 + c   ⇒   c = −25

Q(t) = 25(1 − e−0.03t) lb

↳ step 2 took six lines. Step 1 took the whole previous page.

Does that answer make sense?

Q(t) = 25(1 − e−0.03t)

At t = 0

Q(0) = 25(1 − 1) = 0  pure water, as we said

As t grows

Q → 25 lb  and it never passes it

Is 25 the right number?

(100 gal)(¼ lb/gal) = 25 lb

So in the long run the tank simply reaches the concentration of what is pouring in. Which is obvious — afterwards.

You could have written that number down before solving anything, and used it to check.

Set dQdt = 0:  34 = 3Q100 ⇒ Q = 25

↳ that is the equilibrium solution, from the second week. It is the answer to “eventually”.

Example 1 — every start ends at 25

The direction field for the tank.

The strokes are flat along one height and nowhere else.

Q = 25 lb  the equilibrium

Start empty and the salt climbs to 25. Start above 25 and it falls to 25.

Nothing crosses that line, and nothing stays away from it.

The tank forgets where it started.

↳ this is the picture from week two, now with a tank attached to it.

Just the equation — call it out

Three paragraphs. I want dQdt and Q(0). Nothing else.

1.  A 300 gal tank of pure water. In: 5 gal/min at 2 lb/gal. Out: 5 gal/min.

2.  400 gal holding 20 lb of salt. In: pure water, 8 gal/min. Out: 8 gal/min.

3.  100 gal of pure water. In: 6 gal/min at 3 lb/gal. Out: 4 gal/min.

1.  dQdt = 10 − Q60,  Q(0) = 0

2.  dQdt = −Q50,  Q(0) = 20  rate in is zero

3.  dQdt = 18 − 2Q50 + t,  Q(0) = 0  V = 100 + 2t

Number 2 catches people. Pure water still flows in — it just brings no salt.

When the rates are not equal

Same tank, same sentence. Only now the two rates do not match.

Four gallons in, two gallons out. The tank is filling.

So the volume is not a constant any more

dVdt = 4 − 2 = 2   ⇒   V(t) = 200 + 2t

Everything else is the same sentence. Rate in minus rate out.

dQdt = (4)(1) − (2)Q200 + 2t

dQdt = 4 − Q100 + t

↳ now p is a function of t. That is Wednesday of week two — you have done this.

Example 2 — a tank that is filling

dQdt = 4 − Q100 + t,   Q(0) = 0

Standard form

Q′ + 1100 + tQ = 4  p = 1/(100 + t)

1  build μ

∫dt100 + t = ln(100 + t)  no bars — it is positive

μ = eln(100 + t) = 100 + t

2  multiply every term

(100 + t)Q′ + Q = 4(100 + t)

3  the left side is one derivative

((100 + t)Q)′ = 4(100 + t)

4  integrate, then divide by μ

(100 + t)Q = 2(100 + t)2 + c

Q = 2(100 + t) + c100 + t

Q(0) = 0:   0 = 200 + c100   ⇒   c = −20000

Q(t) = 2(100 + t) − 20000100 + t

↳ no equilibrium this time. The tank is filling, so nothing settles.

Example 2 — the tank holds 500 gallons

Q(t) = 2(100 + t) − 20000100 + t

When does it overflow?

200 + 2t = 500   ⇒   t = 150 min

How much salt is in it then?

Q(150) = 2(250) − 20000250

= 500 − 80 = 420 lb

And the concentration at that moment

420 lb500 gal = 0.84 lb/gal

Which is below 1 lb/gal, the concentration coming in. It has to be — it started from pure water and never catches up.

The model is only valid for 0 ≤ t ≤ 150.

↳ after that the tank is full and the words of the problem no longer describe it.

Example 3 — the feed is being turned down

cin(t) = e−t/10 lb/gal  the same 100 gal tank, 5 gal/min each way, starting from pure water

Somebody is closing the salt valve. At t = 0 the feed is 1 lb/gal. Ten minutes later it is 0.37, and it keeps falling.

Rate in — and it is no longer a number

(5 gal/min)(e−t/10 lb/gal) = 5e−t/10 lb/min

Rate out — unchanged

(5 gal/min)(Q100 lb/gal) = Q20 lb/min

dQdt = 5e−t/10 − Q20,   Q(0) = 0

Same law, same two brackets, same tank. One thing is different.

g(t) = 5e−t/10  not a constant

Every model today so far had a constant on the right. This is the first that does not.

↳ so there is no equilibrium, and “what happens eventually” needs a new answer.

Example 3 — solving it

dQdt = 5e−t/10 − Q20,   Q(0) = 0

Standard form

Q′ + 120Q = 5e−t/10  p = 1/20, g = 5e−t/10

1  build μ — from p only, never from g

μ = e∫dt/20 = et/20

2  multiply every term

et/20Q′ + 120et/20Q = 5 et/20 e−t/10

3  left side is one derivative — and the right side collapses

t20 − t10 = −t20  add the exponents

(et/20Q)′ = 5 e−t/20

4  integrate, then divide by μ

et/20Q = −100 e−t/20 + c  5 ÷ (−1/20) = −100

Q = −100 e−t/10 + c e−t/20  divide both terms

Q(0) = 0:   0 = −100 + c   ⇒   c = 100

Q(t) = 100(e−t/20 − e−t/10)

Example 3 — when is there most salt in the tank?

Q(t) = 100(e−t/20 − e−t/10)

It starts empty, and it ends empty. So somewhere in between there is a maximum.

Do not differentiate that answer

the equation already gives you dQdt

5e−t/10 − Q20 = 0   ⇒   Q = 100e−t/10

So the peak is where the solution meets that curve.

100(e−t/20 − e−t/10) = 100e−t/10

e−t/20 = 2e−t/10   ⇒   et/20 = 2

t = 20 ln 2 ≈ 13.9 min

e−t/20 = 12,   e−t/10 = 14

Qmax = 100(12 − 14) = 25 lb

↳ both numbers are exact. And neither could be read from the equation alone.

Example 3 — no flat line anywhere

Compare this field with the first one. Look for the flat strokes.

They are not all at one height any more. They drift down as time passes.

The solution climbs while the feed is strong, then turns over and washes out.

peak 25 lb at t = 20 ln 2 ≈ 13.9 min

And it goes to zero, not to 25. Eventually there is no salt coming in at all.

A non-constant g means no equilibrium to head for.

↳ the tank still forgets where it started. It just has nowhere to settle.

Newton’s law of cooling

A hot object in a cool room. How fast does it cool?

Newton’s observation: the rate is proportional to how far the object is from the room.

dTdt = −k(T − Ta)

Ta is the room. k > 0 depends on the cup, not on the coffee.

Check the sign, do not memorize it

T > Ta  ⇒  dTdt < 0  hot object cools

T < Ta  ⇒  dTdt > 0  cold object warms

Expand it and it is the tank again.

dTdt + kT = kTa  linear, constant coefficient

↳ different words, same equation, and you already know its shape.

Example 4 — coffee, with two measurements

Coffee at 200°F, room at 70°F. After 10 minutes it is 150°F.  find T(t)

The equation, and the general solution

dTdt = −k(T − 70)

T = 70 + A e−kt  separable, or linear — either way

The first measurement gives A

T(0) = 200:   200 = 70 + A   ⇒   A = 130

The second measurement gives k

T(10) = 150:   150 = 70 + 130 e−10k

e−10k = 80130 = 813

k = 110 ln 138 ≈ 0.0486 per min

T(t) = 70 + 130(813)t/10

↳ two unknowns, so you need two pieces of data. That is why the problem gave you two.

Example 4 — when is it drinkable?

T(t) = 70 + 130(813)t/10  say drinkable means 100°F

70 + 130(813)t/10 = 100

(813)t/10 = 30130 = 313

Logarithm of both sides

t10 ln 813 = ln 313

t = 10 · ln(3/13)ln(8/13) = 10 · −1.4663−0.4855

t ≈ 30 minutes

Both logarithms are negative, and the negatives cancel. If your answer is negative, you divided the wrong way round.

And it never reaches 70°F. It only gets arbitrarily close.

↳ the room temperature is the equilibrium, exactly like the 25 lb in the tank.

Example 4 — the room is the equilibrium

The field for the coffee. Time in minutes, temperature in °F.

Steep at the top, and flatter as it comes down.

T = 70°F  the room

The coffee falls toward the room. A cold drink below the line rises toward it.

Neither one arrives. Which is why “how long until it is exactly 70” has no answer.

Same picture as the tank, with different labels.

↳ that is not a coincidence. Next page.

Example 5 — a falling object, and how far it falls

m dvdt = mg − γv,  m = 10 kg, γ = 2 kg/s, v(0) = 0

Gravity pulls down with mg. Air resistance pushes back, and it grows with the speed.

Rate of change = rate in − rate out, again

10 v′ = 98 − 2v   ⇒   v′ + 0.2 v = 9.8

You have solved this shape twice already today

v(t) = 49(1 − e−0.2t) m/s

The equilibrium has a name here

v′ = 0  ⇒  v = mgγ = 982 = 49 m/s

terminal velocity = mg/γ

It never reaches it. After 15 seconds it is at 95% of it, and that is close enough for a skydiver.

And now a question the tank could not ask

x(t) = ∫v dt = 49t + 245(e−0.2t − 1)

x(10) = 490 + 245(0.135 − 1) ≈ 278 m

↳ the answer to the equation was the speed. The question asked for a distance.

Three problems, one equation

tank:     Q′ = 34 − 0.03 Q

coffee:  T′ = 70k − kT

falling: v′ = 9.8 − 0.2 v

and in general

y′ = a − by,   b > 0

One solution covers all of them

y(t) = ab + (y0 − ab)e−bt

The equilibrium is a/b. 25 lb, 70°F, 49 m/s. Three different worlds, one division.

Everything approaches it, and the starting value only decides from which side.

b sets the speed, a/b sets the destination

Two of today’s examples broke it. Example 2: b depended on t. Example 3: a depended on t.

↳ either one is enough to destroy the destination. That is what the formula needs constants for.

Equilibrium only — call it out

Do not solve anything. Set the derivative to zero.

1.  y′ = 10 − 2y

2.  T′ = −0.2(T − 65)

3.  Q′ = 2 − Q50

4.  Q′ = 4 − Q100 + t

1.  y = 5

2.  T = 65  read it straight from the bracket

3.  Q = 100

4.  none  t is in the equation, so there is no constant solution

Number 4 is the filling tank. It never settles.

Four checks before you hand it in

  1. Units. Every term the same. lb per minute, or degrees per minute.
  2. Sign. Does a hot cup cool, and an empty tank fill?
  3. Limit. Compare with the equilibrium you can read from the equation.
  4. The question. Read it again. It usually asks for a time, or an amount, not for Q(t).

Number 4 is the one that costs marks most often.

A perfect solution of the differential equation, with the final question left unanswered, is not a complete answer.

Finish with a sentence, and put the units in it.

↳ “The tank holds 420 lb of salt when it fills, after 150 minutes.”
