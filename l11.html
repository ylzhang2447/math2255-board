Three claims — true or false?

1.  For y′ = ry with r > 0, every positive start grows without bound.

2.  A solution of an autonomous equation can cross an equilibrium.

3.  A stable equilibrium collects solutions from both sides.

1.  true  y = y0ert; the phase line has one wall, at 0, and it pushes away

2.  false  a wall; uniqueness

3.  true  arrows in, in

Number 1 is the problem. No population does that.

Exponential growth, and why it cannot last

The simplest population model

dydt = ry  growth proportional to size

y = y0ert,   doubles every ln 2r

The growth rate per individual is y′/y = r. A constant. Every rabbit breeds at the same rate no matter how crowded the field is.

The fix

Let the per-individual rate fall as the population grows. The simplest way: a straight line that reaches zero at some size K.

y′y = r(1 − yK)  full rate when y is small, zero when y = K

dydt = r(1 − yK)y

↳ the logistic equation. Verhulst, 1838. Every ecology textbook starts here.

The logistic equation

dydt = r(1 − yK)y  r > 0, K > 0

The two parameters

r is the intrinsic growth rate: the per-individual rate when there is plenty of room.

K is the carrying capacity: the size the environment can support.

Multiply it out

f(y) = ry − rKy2  a parabola, opening down

Step 1 — equilibria

r(1 − y/K)y = 0  ⇒  y = 0  or  y = K

Autonomous, so Friday’s five steps apply. And f is a polynomial, so the theorem holds everywhere: no bad set.

Two walls: an empty world, and a full one.

↳ steps 2 to 4 on the next page.

Example 1 — the phase line of the logistic equation

dydt = r(1 − yK)y

2  sign of f = r(1 − y/K)y

0 < y < K:  (+)(+)(+) = +  ·  y > K:  (+)(−)(+) = −

y < 0:  (+)(+)(−) = −  not a population, but the line does not know that

3  the line: down, up, down

between 0 and K everything rises; above K everything falls

4  classify

y = 0: out, out  ⇒  unstable

y = K: in, in  ⇒  asymptotically stable

Every positive start ends at K.

↳ that is the fate. Step 5, the shape, needs one more idea.

The graph of f — a parabola with its top at K/2

Horizontal axis y, vertical f(y). Here r = 1, K = 4.

Zeros at 0 and K. Crosses up at 0: unstable. Crosses down at K: stable. Friday’s reading.

the top is at y = K/2, where f = rK/4

So the population grows fastest when it is half full. Small populations grow slowly because there are few breeders; large ones because there is little room.

Fastest growth at K/2. Remember where the top is.

↳ the top of f is where the solution curve bends. Next page says why.

Where the curve bends — the second derivative

dydt = f(y)  any autonomous equation

Differentiate both sides with respect to t

d2ydt2 = ddtf(y)

= f′(y) · dydt  chain rule: y depends on t

= f′(y) · f(y)  replace dy/dt by the equation

y″ = f′(y) f(y)

The sign of y″ is the sign of a product. No t anywhere: read it from y alone.

For the logistic equation

f′(y) = r(1 − 2yK)  + below K/2, − above

0 < y < K/2:  (+)(+)  ⇒  y″ > 0,  concave up

K/2 < y < K:  (−)(+)  ⇒  y″ < 0,  concave down

y > K:  (−)(−)  ⇒  y″ > 0,  concave up

Inflection where y = K/2: the top of f.

↳ a curve that starts low is an S. It steepens until half full, then flattens toward K.

Example 1 — the S-curve

r = 1, K = 4. Walls at 0 and at K.

Five starts. From 0.15 and 0.6: an S. Slow, then steep through y = 2, then flat as it nears 4.

From 2, exactly half full: no concave-up part. It starts at the inflection and only flattens.

From 3.5: rises and flattens. From 5.4: falls to 4, concave up the whole way.

every curve above K/2 is concave down; every curve below it is concave up

Step 5 done properly: monotone, walls, and now the bend.

↳ the recipe for a logistic problem, then the exact solution.

The recipe — reading a logistic problem

  1. Read r and K from the equation. Write the equation as r(1 − y/K)y if it is not already.
  2. Equilibria 0 and K; the line is down, up, down; 0 unstable, K stable.
  3. Fate: every positive start → K.
  4. Shape: inflection at K/2; fastest growth there, at rate rK/4.
  5. Sketch the S from y0.

And you can always check

The logistic equation is separable, and this time the integral is worth doing: there is a closed formula.

With the formula you can answer when: when does the population reach half of K? three quarters?

The line gives the fate and the shape. The formula gives the times.

↳ the formula takes two pages. Every line is written.

Example 2 — solving the logistic equation, part 1

dydt = r(1 − yK)y,   y(0) = y0  0 < y0 < K

Separate

dy(1 − y/K)y = r dt

Partial fractions on the left

1(1 − y/K)y = 1y + 1/K1 − y/K

check:  (1 − y/K) + y/K(1 − y/K)y = 1(1 − y/K)y  ✓

Integrate both sides

∫dyy + ∫(1/K) dy1 − y/K = ∫r dt

ln y − ln(1 − y/K) = rt + c  both positive, no bars

ln y1 − y/K = rt + c

y1 − y/K = C ert,   C = ec

↳ implicit so far. Next page: the constant, then solve for y.

Example 2 — part 2: the constant, and y itself

y1 − y/K = C ert,   y(0) = y0

Initial condition

t = 0:  C = y01 − y0/K = Ky0K − y0

Solve for y

y = Cert(1 − y/K)  ⇒  y(1 + Cert/K) = Cert

y = Cert1 + Cert/K = KCKe−rt + C

put C in and multiply top and bottom by K − y0

y(t) = y0Ky0 + (K − y0)e−rt

Check it against the phase line

t = 0:  y0Ky0 + K − y0 = y0  ✓

t → ∞:  e−rt → 0,  y → y0Ky0 = K  ✓

y0 = K:  y = K for all t  ✓

↳ the formula agrees with the line at every point we can check. Now it does what the line cannot.

Example 3 — a fish population, with numbers

dydt = 0.5(1 − y8000)y,   y(0) = 1000  time in years

Read it

r = 0.5 per year,  K = 8000,  y0 = 1000

fate: y → 8000  ·  fastest growth at 4000, at 0.5 · 8000/4 = 1000 fish/yr

The formula

y = 8 000 0001000 + 7000 e−0.5t = 80001 + 7e−0.5t

When is it half full?

80001 + 7e−0.5t = 4000  ⇒  1 + 7e−0.5t = 2

e−0.5t = 17  ⇒  t = 2 ln 7 ≈ 3.9 years

When is it three quarters full?

80001 + 7e−0.5t = 6000  ⇒  1 + 7e−0.5t = 43

e−0.5t = 121  ⇒  t = 2 ln 21 ≈ 6.1 years

From 1000 to 4000: 3.9 yr.  From 4000 to 6000: 2.2 yr more.

The second stretch is shorter although it is smaller: it happens near the top of f.

↳ the line said “fastest at 4000”. The formula puts a clock on it.

Call it out — y′ = 2(1 − y/10)y

1.  r and K?

2.  Start at 1: where does it end? Start at 15?

3.  At what size is growth fastest, and how fast?

4.  Start at 7: concave up or down?

1.  r = 2, K = 10

2.  10 and 10  from below and from above

3.  at 5, rate rK/4 = 5

4.  down  7 is above K/2 = 5 and below K

↳ four answers, no formula needed. Now change one sign.

A threshold — the same equation with the sign changed

dydt = −r(1 − yT)y

Some populations need a minimum size

Too few to find mates, too few to defend against predators. Below some T the population shrinks.

2  sign of f = −r(1 − y/T)y

0 < y < T:  (−)(+)(+) = −  ·  y > T:  (−)(−)(+) = +

3, 4  up, down, up

0: in, in  stable  ·  T: out, out  unstable

Below T: extinction.  Above T: growth without bound.

↳ the logistic line upside down. T is a threshold, not a capacity.

Example 4 — above the threshold it does not just grow, it blows up

dydt = −r(1 − yT)y,   y(0) = y0

The same algebra, with r → −r and K → T

y(t) = y0Ty0 + (T − y0)ert

y0 < T:  T − y0 > 0, the denominator grows  ⇒  y → 0

y0 > T:  T − y0 < 0, the denominator reaches zero when

ert = y0y0 − T  ⇒  t* = 1r ln y0y0 − T

Above T the solution reaches infinity at a finite time t*.

With numbers: r = 0.25, T = 100

y0 = 80:  dies out  ·  y0 = 100:  stays

y0 = 120:  t* = 4 ln 12020 = 4 ln 6 ≈ 7.2 years

The line said “up without limit”. It could not say “in seven years”.

↳ unbounded growth is not a model of anything for long. So put a ceiling back.

Example 4 — two fates, one threshold

r = 0.5, T = 2. Walls at 0 and at T.

From 0.5 and 1.5: both sink to 0. Slowly at first for the one near T.

From 2.25 and 2.6: both climb, and the climb steepens. Each one leaves the top of the picture at a finite time.

a wall that pushes away on both sides, and nothing above it to stop the rise

Unstable at T, and no ceiling.

↳ now the model with both a floor and a ceiling.

Example 5 — logistic growth with a threshold

dydt = −r(1 − yT)(1 − yK)y

1  equilibria

y = 0,  y = T,  y = K  0 < T < K

2  sign of f = −r(1 − y/T)(1 − y/K)y

0 < y < T:  (−)(+)(+)(+) = −

T < y < K:  (−)(−)(+)(+) = +

y > K:  (−)(−)(−)(+) = −

3, 4  up, down, up, down

0 stable  ·  T unstable  ·  K stable

Below T: extinction.  Above T: settles at K.

↳ three walls, and the middle one is the one that matters.

Example 5 — a hair below T and a hair above

T = 1, K = 3. Three walls.

From 0.95: extinction. From 1.05: recovery to K. The two starts differ by one tenth.

From 0.6: extinction. From 1.8: an S up to K. From 4: down to K.

the threshold is a dividing line; the capacity is a basin

Conservation biology in one picture: keep the population above T.

Example 6 — harvesting: take h fish per year out of the lake

dydt = r(1 − yK)y − h

What changed

f(y) = r(1 − y/K)y − h  the parabola, moved down

Equilibria: where the parabola equals h

r(1 − y/K)y = h  ⇔  rKy2 − ry + h = 0

y = K2 ± K2√1 − 4h/(rK)

h < rK/4: two  ·  h = rK/4: one  ·  h > rK/4: none

Read the line

h < rK/4:  lower point unstable, upper point stable

h > rK/4:  f < 0 everywhere  ⇒  extinction

rK/4 is the largest harvest the lake can sustain.

Example 3:  0.5 · 8000/4 = 1000 fish per year

↳ the top of f once more. Fish faster than that, and the next picture shows what happens.

Example 6 — the harvest line against the parabola

The same parabola, r = 1, K = 4. Its top is at height rK/4 = 1.

The dashed lines are two harvest rates. Equilibria are where a line meets the parabola: there f − h = 0.

h = 0.6:  two crossings, at about 0.7 and 3.3

Between them the parabola is above the line, so the population grows: the upper crossing is stable, the lower one is a threshold.

h = 1.2:  the line clears the parabola. No crossing. f − h < 0 everywhere.

Above the top of the parabola, every start dies.

↳ the model is chosen by the walls the story needs. Three claims, then the card.

Three more — true or false?

1.  A logistic population that starts above K decreases to K.

2.  The S-curve bends at y = K.

3.  Harvesting at any constant rate below rK/4 leaves a stable equilibrium.

1.  true  above K the arrow points down; K is a wall

2.  false  at K/2, the top of f

3.  true  the upper crossing; but the lower one is a new threshold to stay above

Number 2 is the one people get wrong on exams.
