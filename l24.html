Chapter 6: a new method

Chapter 3 had limits

Undetermined coefficients works only for exponentials, polynomials, sines and cosines.

In applications the forcing can switch on and off, or act only for a short time. Guessing does not handle that.

The idea of chapter 6

The Laplace transform turns a function of t into a function of s.

Why it helps

It turns a derivative into multiplication by s. So a differential equation becomes algebra.

We solve the algebra, then transform back.

Today: what the transform is, and the first few transforms.

The Laplace transform

ℒ{f} = F(s) = ∫0∞e−stf(t) dt

Reading the formula

Input: a function f(t), for t ≥ 0.

Multiply by e−st and integrate in t. The t disappears.

Output: a function F(s) of a new variable s.

We use a capital letter for the transform: f → F, y → Y.

The upper limit is ∞

This is an improper integral. To compute transforms we first need its precise definition.

The factor e−st decays when s > 0. It makes the improper integral converge.

The transform exists only for the s where the integral converges.

↳ what an integral up to ∞ means.

Improper integrals

∫a∞f(t) dt = limA→∞ ∫aAf(t) dt

Definition

Suppose the ordinary integral ∫aAf(t) dt exists for every A > a.

If the limit exists and is a finite number, the improper integral converges, and its value is the limit.

If the limit is ±∞ or does not exist, the integral diverges.

An integral up to ∞ is not an ordinary integral. It is defined as this limit.

To compute it

1. Replace ∞ by A.

2. Compute the ordinary integral from a to A.

3. Take the limit as A → ∞.

↳ step 3 needs limits like these.

Example 1 — ∫0∞ectdt

∫0∞ect dt,   c a constant

Steps 1 and 2 (c ≠ 0)

∫0Aectdt = 1cect|0A = 1c(ecA − 1)

Step 3

c < 0:  ecA → 0,  so the integral is 1c(0 − 1) = −1c

c > 0:  ecA → ∞:  diverges

c = 0:  ∫0A1 dt = A → ∞:  diverges

∫0∞ectdt = −1c  only when c < 0

for example  ∫0∞e−2tdt = 12

A decaying exponential has finite area under it; a growing one does not.

↳ which functions have a transform?

When does ℒ{f} exist?

Theorem 6.1.2

Two conditions on f

Piecewise continuous: only finitely many jumps on any finite interval.

Exponential order: |f(t)| ≤ Keat for large t.

Then ℒ{f} exists for s > a.

What this means

f may jump, but it may not grow faster than some exponential.

Then e−stf(t) decays when s > a, and the integral converges.

1, t5, e4t, sin t:  all fine

et2:  grows too fast, no transform

↳ the first transforms, straight from the definition.

Example 6 — ℒ{t2}

ℒ{t2} = ∫0∞t2e−stdt,   s > 0

Example 7 — ℒ{sin at}

F(s) = ∫0∞e−st sin at dt,   s > 0

By parts: u = sin at, dv = e−stdt

F = [−1se−st sin at]0∞ + as∫0∞e−st cos at dt

the bracket is 0 − 0 = 0

By parts again: u = cos at

∫0∞e−st cos at dt = 1s − asF

So F appears on both sides

F = as(1s − asF) = as2 − a2s2F

(1 + a2s2)F = as2  ⇒  (s2 + a2)F = a

ℒ{sin at} = as2 + a2,   s > 0

Integrating by parts twice brings F back; then we solve for F.

↳ cosine, using what we just found.

Example 8 — ℒ{cos at}

ℒ{cos at} = ∫0∞e−st cos at dt,   s > 0

By parts: u = cos at

= [−1se−st cos at]0∞ − as∫0∞e−st sin at dt

the bracket is 0 − (−1s) = 1s;   cos 0 = 1

= 1s − as·as2 + a2 = s2 + a2 − a2s(s2 + a2)

ℒ{cos at} = ss2 + a2

Sine has a on top; cosine has s on top. The bottom is the same.

↳ adding transforms.

Linearity

ℒ{c1f1 + c2f2} = c1ℒ{f1} + c2ℒ{f2}

Why

∫0∞e−st(c1f1 + c2f2)dt = c1∫0∞e−stf1dt + c2∫0∞e−stf2dt

The integral of a sum is the sum of the integrals; constants come out.

In practice

Split a sum into pieces, transform each piece, and keep the constants in front.

But ℒ{fg} is not ℒ{f}ℒ{g}.

ℒ{1·1} = 1s,  but  ℒ{1}ℒ{1} = 1s2

↳ an example.

The card — the Laplace transform

ℒ{f} = F(s) = ∫0∞e−stf(t) dt

Improper integral: replace ∞ by A, integrate, let A → ∞.

Exists for s > a when f is piecewise continuous and of exponential order a.
