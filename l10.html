Autonomous equations

dydt = f(y)  no t on the right-hand side

You have met many

Q′ = 34 − 3Q100  ·  T′ = −k(T − 70)  ·  y′ = y2

not autonomous:  y′ = t − 2y/t,  y′ = 2t y2

The rule does not look at the clock. Only at where you are.

Two consequences

The direction field is the same in every column. Slide a solution left or right, and it is still a solution.

It is always separable. But ∫dy/f(y) is usually ugly, and today we refuse to compute it.

Everything today is read from the sign of f(y).

↳ first: why the sign is enough.

Why the sign of f(y) is enough

dydt = f(y),   f and f′ continuous

Equilibrium solutions

f(c) = 0  ⇒  y(t) = c is a solution  0 = f(c) ✓

Monday: nothing else may cross the line y = c

Between two equilibria

f has no zero there  ⇒  one sign on the whole strip

f > 0  ⇒  y′ > 0  ⇒  y goes up, the whole time

And where does it go?

up, but it can never reach the wall above  no crossing

up and bounded  ⇒  it approaches the wall  closer and closer, forever

no wall above  ⇒  up without limit

Every solution is monotone, and its fate is the nearest wall in its direction.

↳ three facts, all from the sign of f. That is the whole section.

The recipe — the phase line

  1. Equilibria. Solve f(y) = 0.
  2. Sign of f on each strip between them. One test point per strip.
  3. Draw the line. A vertical y-axis, a dot at each equilibrium, an arrow up where f > 0, down where f < 0.
  4. Classify each dot by its arrows.
  5. Sketch y(t): monotone curves that approach the walls and never cross them.

Step 4, the three kinds

arrows in from both sides:  asymptotically stable

arrows out on both sides:  unstable

in on one side, out on the other:  semistable

And you can always check: draw the direction field, or solve it if the integral is friendly.

↳ the first example has a friendly integral. We will not use it.

Example 1

dydt = y2 − 4

1  equilibria

y2 − 4 = (y − 2)(y + 2) = 0  ⇒  y = −2, 2

2  sign of f, one test point per strip

y = −3:  f = 5 > 0  ·  y = 0:  f = −4 < 0  ·  y = 3:  f = 5 > 0

3  the line:  below −2 up toward it  ·  between: down, toward −2 and away from 2  ·  above 2: up, away

4  classify

y = −2: arrows in from both sides  ⇒  asymptotically stable

y = 2: arrows out on both sides  ⇒  unstable

↳ no integral. Now the picture the line predicts.

The words, precisely

Asymptotically stable

Start close enough to c, and the solution stays close and tends to c as t → ∞.

Example 1:  y = −2  arrows in, in

Unstable

Some starts, as close to c as you like, move away from it.

Example 1:  y = 2  arrows out, out

Semistable

Stable from one side, unstable from the other. Not one of the book’s two definitions, but all over its problem sets.

The line makes the words visible: in, in — out, out — in, out.

On an exam: the word, and the arrows that justify it.

Example 2 — four strips

dydt = (y2 − y − 2)(1 − y)2

1  equilibria — factor first

(y − 2)(y + 1)(1 − y)2 = 0  ⇒  y = −1, 1, 2

2  sign of f — four strips, four test points

y = −2:  (−)(−)(+) = +  ·  y = 0:  (−)(+)(+) = −

y = 1.5:  (−)(+)(+) = −  ·  y = 3:  (+)(+)(+) = +

3  the line: up, down, down, up

the square never changes sign, so f does not change sign at 1

4  classify

−1: in, in  stable  ·  1: in from above, out below  semistable  ·  2: out, out  unstable

↳ a double root gives a semistable point. That is the pattern to remember.

Example 2 — what semistable looks like

Three walls. Six starts.

From −2 and from −0.5: both go to −1. Stable: it collects from both sides.

From 0.6: down to −1. From 1.9: down to 1. The wall at 1 collects from above only.

From 2.15 and 2.5: up without limit. Unstable: 2 pushes everything away.

sit exactly at 1 and you stay; sit a hair below and you fall to −1

Semistable: a wall you can lean on from one side only.

↳ one more example, and this one cannot be factored.

Example 3 — a sign you cannot factor

dydt = ey − 1 − y

1  equilibria

ey − 1 − y = 0  ⇔  ey = 1 + y

the line 1 + y is tangent to ey at y = 0, and below it everywhere else

so the only zero is y = 0

2  sign of f — from the same picture

ey ≥ 1 + y for every y  ⇒  f ≥ 0 everywhere

f > 0 below 0  and  f > 0 above 0

3, 4  the line: up, up

0: in from below, out above  ⇒  semistable

start below 0: climbs to 0  ·  start above 0: climbs without limit

One equilibrium, and it is semistable.

A sign found by an inequality, not by a factor. The graph of f would show it at once: it touches the axis at 0.

↳ and the picture agrees.

Example 3 — a floor from below, a launch pad from above

Below the axis every stroke points up, gently. Above it, up and steeper.

One wall, at y = 0.

From −3, −1.5, −0.4: up to 0, never across. From 0.3 and 0.9: up, slowly at first, then off the top.

a floor for everything below, a launch pad for everything above

Semistable, and only one equilibrium in the whole plane.

↳ now the other way to read all of this: the graph of f.

The graph of f(y) says the same thing

Horizontal axis: y. Vertical: f(y). No t anywhere.

Zeros of the graph: the equilibria. Above the axis: arrows up. Below: arrows down.

crosses from + to − going right:  stable  −1

crosses from − to +:  unstable  2

touches without crossing:  semistable  1

One graph, no test points, every equilibrium classified.

What the line tells you, and what it cannot

It tells you

The fate of every start: which wall, or off to infinity.

Which equilibria a real system can sit at. A semistable or unstable one is a pencil balanced on its point.

last Friday’s tank:  f(Q) = 34 − 3Q100,  zero at 25, + below, − above  ⇒  stable

That is why every tank forgot its start. Now it has a name.

It cannot tell you

How long anything takes. For that you solve.

Whether “up without limit” means forever or a finite time. y′ = y climbs forever; y′ = y2 reaches infinity at t = 1/y0. Same arrow.

The shape of the curve in between. Monday: where it bends.

The line is about destinations, not about the trip.

Call it out — in Example 2, where does it go?

y′ = (y − 2)(y + 1)(1 − y)2  walls at −1, 1, 2

1.  y(0) = 0

2.  y(0) = 1.5

3.  y(0) = −5

4.  y(0) = 2.01

5.  y(0) = 2

2.  → 1  down, and 1 is the first wall

3.  → −1  up, from below

4.  → ∞  a hair above 2 is enough

5.  stays at 2  forever, by uniqueness

Three claims — true or false?

1.  A solution of an autonomous equation can go up for a while and then come back down.

2.  A solution can reach an equilibrium in finite time.

3.  Two neighboring equilibria can both be asymptotically stable.

1.  false  turning needs y′ = 0, which only happens on a wall

2.  false  reaching it is touching it: two solutions through one point

3.  false  between them f has one sign, so the arrow leaves one of them

All three false. All three are Monday’s theorem in disguise.
